Emergency money and counter-money, 1967–1970.

A research project on the Bank of Biafra, the Central Bank of Nigeria, and what happens to a currency when the issuing authority's territorial control is contested.

By Bamidele Aly In preparation May 2026

The question

The Nigerian Civil War of 1967–1970 produced a brief and contested second issuer of currency on Nigerian soil. The Republic of Biafra, declaring independence in May 1967, issued its own pound notes and coins in 1968 and again in 1969. The Central Bank of Nigeria responded by demonetising the existing Nigerian pound in January 1968 and reissuing new notes that the Biafran administration could not redeem.

This sequence — secessionist currency, federal counter-currency, and the consequent split between what circulated and what was legal tender — raises a question the literature on currency boards and central bank independence does not handle cleanly: what does monetary sovereignty mean when the territorial perimeter of the issuing authority is itself the matter in dispute?

Sources

The project draws on four bodies of material. The first is the surviving numismatic record: the Biafran issues themselves, the pre-war Nigerian pound, and the post-1968 Nigerian replacement. Mint dates, signature blocks and security features establish the timeline.

The second is institutional correspondence — Bank of England archives, Central Bank of Nigeria publications and internal memoranda where they have been released, and the Mission historique de la Banque de France collection where European reserve banks recorded their reading of the conflict. The third is contemporary press: Nigerian, British and French papers reported the demonetisation, the Biafran issues and the smuggling of pre-1968 notes across the lines.

The fourth is memoirs and oral history from officials on both sides. The literature here is partial — much of what was decided was decided informally, and the people who knew most have either passed or never written — but the gaps are themselves informative about how decisions about the currency were taken.

Approach

The argument the paper develops is that the Biafran issuance and the federal counter-issuance are best read together, not separately, and that they form a useful comparative case for other twentieth-century episodes of contested currency. Spanish Civil War regional issuance, partition-era India, occupied-zone Germany 1945–48 and post-Soviet Caucasus currencies each share structural features with the Biafran case: a recognised pre-existing currency, a contested second issuer, and a state with formal authority but incomplete territorial control.

Reading the Nigerian case in that comparative frame makes visible what the West African Currency Board literature, which tends to stop at independence in 1960, has not addressed: the period from 1960 to 1970 in which the institutional architecture of Nigerian central banking is still being established under conditions of civil war.

Status & venues

The paper is in preparation, with submissions intended during 2026 to economic history and African studies venues. The framing sits alongside my earlier published work on monetary sovereignty in Ghana and Nigeria in the late 1950s and on the introduction of the West African sterling in Southern Nigeria in 1916.
